Spring in Southeast Michigan is unpredictable. One week it is 60 degrees and sunny, the next it snows. But once the frost clears and the ground firms up — typically mid-March to early April — it is time to start preparing your lawn for the growing season.

After 25 years of managing [turf across Oakland and Macomb Counties](/services/turf), we have dialed in the exact sequence that gives Michigan lawns the strongest possible start. Here is your step-by-step spring checklist.

## Step 1: Spring Cleanup (Late March to Early April)

Before you do anything else, clear the debris that accumulated over winter:

- **Rake leaves and twigs.** Matted leaves smother grass and create conditions for snow mold and fungal disease.
- **Remove fallen branches.** Michigan ice storms and heavy snow leave plenty behind.
- **Clear garden beds.** Pull dead annuals and cut back perennial stems to make room for new growth.
- **Pick up pet waste.** It sounds obvious, but winter buildup creates nitrogen burn spots if left in place.

Do not rush this step. Walking on frozen or waterlogged turf causes compaction. Wait until the ground is firm and dry enough that your footprints do not leave visible impressions.

## Step 2: Inspect and Repair Damage

Walk your entire property and look for:

- **Snow plow damage.** Torn turf edges along driveways and sidewalks are common. Replace sod or overseed these spots early so they fill in before summer.
- **Bare patches.** Winter kill, salt damage, and heavy traffic leave thin or dead spots. Scratch the soil surface, spread seed, and keep it moist.
- **Drainage issues.** Note any areas where water pools after rain. These spots may need grading or a [drainage solution](/services/irrigation) before they become chronic problems.
- **Mole and vole damage.** Raised tunnels and surface runs are common in spring. Tamp them down with your foot and overseed as needed.

## Step 3: First Mow (Mid to Late April)

Your first mow of the season is more about cleanup than cutting. Set your mower to 3 inches and make one pass to even things out. This removes dead tips and encourages the grass to start tillering, which is how it thickens.

**Sharpen your mower blade first.** A dull blade tears grass instead of cutting it cleanly, which stresses the plant and creates a brown, ragged appearance. Sharpen or replace the blade at the start of every season.

**Do not scalp the lawn.** Cutting too short in spring exposes soil to sunlight, which encourages crabgrass germination. Keep cool-season grass at 3 to 3.5 inches throughout the growing season.

## Step 4: Apply Pre-Emergent Herbicide (Mid-April)

Timing is everything with pre-emergent weed control. In Southeast Michigan, the target window is when soil temperatures reach 55 degrees for several consecutive days. This usually falls in mid-April but can vary by a week or two depending on the year.

Pre-emergent creates a chemical barrier at the soil surface that prevents crabgrass and other annual weed seeds from germinating. Apply it before the forsythia bushes finish blooming — that is the traditional Michigan timing indicator.

**Important:** If you plan to overseed thin spots, do not apply pre-emergent in those areas. It will prevent grass seed from germinating too. Spot-treat those areas with post-emergent later in the season.

## Step 5: First Fertilizer Application (Late April to Early May)

Michigan lawns benefit from a balanced, slow-release fertilizer in late spring. A product with a ratio near 20-5-10 (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) works well for most cool-season grass blends.

Apply when the grass is actively growing and the threat of hard frost has passed. Fertilizing too early wastes product because dormant roots cannot absorb nutrients efficiently.

If you had your lawn [aerated in fall](/blog/when-to-aerate-lawn-michigan), the root system is already in great shape to take up spring fertilizer.

## Step 6: Check Your Irrigation System (Early May)

Before you need it, turn on your [irrigation system](/services/irrigation) and run each zone:

- **Check for broken heads.** Freeze damage and snow plows are the usual culprits.
- **Adjust spray patterns.** Make sure heads are not watering the sidewalk or house siding.
- **Test coverage.** Set out tuna cans or rain gauges across each zone. You want 1 to 1.5 inches of water per week, applied in 2 to 3 sessions.
- **Inspect the backflow preventer.** This is required by code in Michigan and should be tested annually.

Starting irrigation too early is a common mistake. Cool spring rain usually provides enough moisture through April. Wait until consistent warm weather arrives before running the system regularly.

## Step 7: Address Broadleaf Weeds (May)

Dandelions, clover, and plantain start appearing in May. A targeted broadleaf herbicide applied when weeds are young and actively growing is far more effective than waiting until they mature.

Spot-spraying individual weeds is more precise and uses less chemical than blanket applications. For organic-focused lawns, hand-pulling while the soil is moist after rain is surprisingly effective if done consistently.

## Step 8: Set Your Mowing Schedule

Once the grass is growing steadily, establish a regular mowing routine:

- **Frequency:** Every 5 to 7 days during peak growth (May and June)
- **Height:** 3 to 3.5 inches for cool-season grass
- **One-third rule:** Never remove more than one-third of the blade height in a single mow
- **Mulch clippings:** Leave clippings on the lawn. They decompose quickly and return nitrogen to the soil.
